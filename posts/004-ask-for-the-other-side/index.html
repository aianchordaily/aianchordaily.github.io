Try This Today #004 · November 26, 2026

Your AI only hears your side of the story. Make it ask for the other one.

TL;DR A Google Research study found that AI assistants give worse social advice when they only hear one person's side, tend to adopt the user's framing, and rarely ask clarifying questions. A simple two-step prompt makes AI ask about the other person before advising you.

The story

Google Research built a test for something assistants are asked to do all the time: work out what another person really wants from one person's one-sided description. The researchers simulated a "target" person with a hidden motive and a simulated user who describes what happened to the assistant. Because they designed the hidden motive, they knew the right answer.

Across 12 models, assistants did worse than when they could see the events directly. They tended to adopt the user's framing and did not reliably ask clarifying questions. The framework and more than 20,000 examples are open-sourced, and the paper is on arXiv. Two caveats: grading was done by another AI model acting as judge, and these are simulated conversations rather than real ones.

We tried it

We tested this in the ChatGPT app with a made-up situation: a roommate who keeps leaving dirty dishes in the sink. Asked normally, ChatGPT took our side straight away and wrote us a script to confront her. In a new chat, we asked the same thing plus one line: "Before you advise me, ask me 3 questions about what she might want or be thinking." This time it asked what she might be dealing with, what she believes about the shared kitchen, and how she might feel. After we answered (night shifts, no chore agreement, she sometimes cooks for both of us), it told us we might be treating an unspoken expectation as a broken agreement, and suggested agreeing on a simple chore plan together instead of a confrontation. This is one informal test, not a study, but it matches what the researchers found.

Why it matters

People increasingly ask AI for help with relationships, work conflicts and family situations. If the assistant mostly mirrors your view back to you, it can make you feel right without helping you be fair, and that matters most just when emotions run high.

What this means for you

Try this prompt sequence the next time you ask for advice about another person:

  1. "Before you advise me, ask me 3 questions about what the other person might want or be thinking."
  2. Answer honestly, including the parts that don't flatter you.
  3. "Now argue the other person's side as fairly as you can."
  4. "Given both sides, what would you suggest I do?"

And keep perspective: for big decisions involving people you care about, a trusted friend who knows everyone involved is still the better advisor.
